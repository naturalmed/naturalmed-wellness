Chronic Stress and Chinese Medicine — When the Body Never Switches Off

Nuno Pestana, BSc TCM · October 2026 · 8 min read

Your mind is always racing and your body tense. Rest doesn't feel restful. Chronic stress isn't just in your head—it's a pattern affecting the whole body that Chinese medicine can help unwind.

Your mind is always one step ahead — planning, worrying, replaying. Your shoulders are permanently tense and even rest doesn't feel restful. Chronic stress is not just "in your head" — Chinese medicine sees it as a pattern the whole body falls into, and one it can help unwind.

How Chinese medicine understands chronic stress

In Chinese medicine, the organ most closely tied to how we respond to stress is the Liver — not the physical organ familiar from Western anatomy, but the Liver as a functional system responsible for the smooth, unobstructed flow of Qi throughout the body. When life demands more than we can process — deadlines, conflict, uncertainty, constant mental noise — that flow becomes constrained. The technical name for this is Liver Qi Stagnation, and it is one of the most common patterns seen in clinic today.

Stagnant Qi does not stay quiet. Left unchecked, it presses against the Spleen and disturbs digestion, rises upward to tighten the neck and jaw, or transforms into Heat that disturbs sleep and mood. This is why chronic stress rarely shows up as one clean symptom — it tends to arrive as a cluster: tight shoulders, an unsettled stomach, irritability, and a mind that will not slow down, all at once.

"Where Liver Qi flows freely, the mood is settled. Where it stagnates, irritability, tension and a hundred small complaints follow."

The most common patterns behind chronic stress

Not everyone under stress presents the same way. In clinical practice, these patterns come up most often:

  • Liver Qi Stagnation — a tight chest or throat, sighing frequently, irritability, premenstrual tension, and symptoms that clearly worsen with emotional pressure. Often the entry point before other patterns develop.
  • Liver Qi stagnation transforming into Heat — the same tension, but sharper: a short fuse, headaches, red eyes, a bitter taste in the mouth, and disrupted sleep. Common after weeks or months of unresolved stress.
  • Spleen Qi deficiency from overthinking — fatigue, poor appetite, a heavy feeling after eating, and a mind that ruminates rather than rests. Frequently seen in people who "think for a living" or worry compulsively.
  • Heart-Kidney disharmony — a wired, anxious exhaustion: tired but unable to switch off, palpitations, and a background hum of unease that does not resolve with sleep alone. Typically the result of stress sustained over a long period.

Identifying which of these is driving your symptoms — rather than treating "stress" as one single thing — is what allows treatment to actually settle the body, not just mask the tension for a few hours.

What treatment looks like

Acupuncture for chronic stress typically focuses on moving Liver Qi and calming the Shen, using points such as Taichong (LR3), Neiguan (PC6) and Yintang, often combined with points chosen for whichever secondary pattern is present — cooling points if Heat has developed, or tonifying points where Spleen or Kidney deficiency is part of the picture. Most patients notice a difference in how "wound up" they feel within the first few sessions, though the underlying pattern usually needs six to eight treatments to properly settle.

Herbal medicine is often prescribed alongside acupuncture. Xiao Yao San (Free and Easy Wanderer), one of the best-known formulas in the Chinese pharmacopoeia, is specifically designed to move Liver Qi and support the Spleen — precisely the combination stress tends to disrupt.

Three things you can try this week

1. Press Taichong (LR3). Find the point on the top of your foot, in the web between the big toe and second toe, about two finger-widths back. Massage firmly in small circles for 1–2 minutes on each foot. This is the primary point for moving stuck Liver Qi and is often the first thing to reach for when stress feels physically "stuck."

2. Build in a daily "off-ramp." Even ten minutes of slow, unhurried movement — walking without your phone, gentle stretching, or simply sitting without a task — gives stagnant Qi somewhere to go. The specific activity matters less than the consistency: the body learns the pattern of release only through repetition.

3. Eat before you are starving, not after. Stress often pushes eating to the edges of the day — skipped or rushed meals followed by a large one late at night. This pattern strains the Spleen further. Regular, unhurried meals — even simple ones — give the digestive system the steadiness it needs to keep supporting you through a demanding period.

When to seek treatment

If stress has become your baseline rather than an occasional visitor, it is worth having it properly assessed rather than waiting for it to resolve on its own. A diagnostic consultation can identify which pattern is at play and set out a plan to help the body find its way back to balance.
