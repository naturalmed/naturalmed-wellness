Menopause and Chinese Medicine — Finding Balance Through the Change

Nuno Pestana, BSc TCM · November 2026 · 8 min read

Hot flushes, night sweats, and mood shifts of menopause are natural yet challenging. For over 2,000 years, Chinese medicine has offered support for this transition.

Hot flushes that arrive without warning. Nights broken by sweats and racing thoughts. A mood that feels less like your own. Menopause is a natural transition, not an illness — but that does not make its symptoms easy to live with. Chinese medicine has described this stage of life for over two thousand years, and offers a way to understand and support it.

How Chinese medicine understands menopause

In Chinese medicine, menopause is not treated as a disease but as a natural change in the body's energy — one of the transitions that mark a woman's life. The symptoms are real and can be disruptive, but they are understood as a sign that the body is rebalancing, not as something that has gone wrong.

The Huangdi Neijing, the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic, describes a woman's life in cycles of seven years. At around forty-nine, the Ren and Chong vessels weaken, Tian Gui — the essence that drives menstruation and fertility — is exhausted, and the periods end.

Paraphrased from the Huangdi Neijing (Suwen, chapter 1)

The organ most closely tied to this transition is the Kidney, which stores the body's deepest reserves of Yin (cooling, moistening, nourishing) and Yang (warming, activating). As those reserves naturally decline, the balance between the two can slip. When Yin falls behind, Yang seems to rise — and heat, restlessness and sweating follow. When Yang weakens, cold, tiredness and low drive take over. Most women show a mixture, and the mixture is different for each woman, which is why two women going through menopause can need very different treatment.

The most common patterns behind menopausal symptoms

Menopause rarely looks the same twice. In clinical practice, these patterns come up most often, usually in combination:

  • Kidney Yin deficiency — hot flushes, night sweats, a warm feeling in the palms, soles and chest, a dry mouth, skin or eyes, and light, restless sleep. The most typical menopausal pattern.
  • Kidney Yang deficiency — feeling cold, tiredness, an aching lower back or knees, needing to pass urine often (especially at night), and low drive. More common later in the transition.
  • Liver Qi stagnation — mood swings, irritability, tender breasts, tension headaches, and symptoms that clearly worsen under stress.
  • Heart–Kidney disharmony — palpitations, anxiety, a racing mind at night and difficulty falling asleep.
  • Spleen deficiency with Dampness — bloating, a heavy feeling, weight gain around the middle and tiredness after meals.

Identifying which of these is behind your symptoms — usually more than one — is what allows treatment to be tailored to you, rather than aimed at "hot flushes" as a single label.

What treatment looks like

Acupuncture for menopause usually combines points that nourish the Kidneys — such as Taixi (KI 3) and Sanyinjiao (SP 6) — with points chosen for the pattern in front of us: Shenmen (HT 7) to settle the mind and sleep, or Taichong (LR 3) where irritability and tension dominate. For the cold, Yang-deficient type, gentle moxibustion (warming with mugwort) can be added; it is not used for hot flushes or Yin-deficient heat. Treatment is usually weekly at first, then spaced out as symptoms settle.

Herbal medicine is often prescribed alongside. Classical formulas include Liu Wei Di Huang Wan (Six-Ingredient Pill with Rehmannia) for Kidney Yin deficiency, Zhi Bai Di Huang Wan when heat signs are strong, Jin Gui Shen Qi Wan (Kidney Qi Pill) for Kidney Yang, and Xiao Yao San (Free and Easy Wanderer) where Liver Qi stagnation drives the mood. Each prescription is built around the individual pattern.

An honest word on the evidence: research on acupuncture and herbs for menopausal symptoms has given mixed results. Many women report feeling better, while trials disagree on how much. We see Chinese medicine as a way to support you alongside the advice of your GP, not instead of it. Always tell your practitioner about any medication you take, including HRT, so that treatment can be planned safely.

Three things you can try this week

1. Cool and moisten from the inside. Favour foods that nourish Yin — pears, black sesame seeds, tofu, mung beans, leafy greens and plenty of water — and go easy on what tends to bring on heat: very spicy food, alcohol and a lot of strong coffee. Keep a short note for a week of what seems to set off your own flushes.

2. Press Sanyinjiao (SP 6). Find the point on the inner leg, about four finger-widths above the tip of the ankle bone, just behind the edge of the shin bone. Press firmly in small circles for 1–2 minutes on each leg, ideally in the evening. This point is traditionally used to nourish Yin and calm the mind. Avoid it if you could be pregnant.

3. Keep the bedroom cool and the evening slow. Keep the room cool, use layers you can take off, and keep the hour before bed free of screens and stimulating conversations. A regular bedtime helps Yin settle, and gentle movement such as walking or qigong earlier in the day does more good than hard exercise late at night.

When to seek help

Menopausal symptoms are common, but some signs need a medical check first: any bleeding after your periods have stopped for twelve months, unusually heavy or prolonged bleeding, new pelvic pain, chest pain or a heartbeat that worries you, or low mood that is persistent or severe. See your GP about these before anything else. If the symptoms are simply wearing you down — the sleep, the flushes, the mood — it is worth having them properly assessed rather than putting up with them. A consultation can identify which pattern is behind them and set out a plan, alongside any medical care you are already receiving.
